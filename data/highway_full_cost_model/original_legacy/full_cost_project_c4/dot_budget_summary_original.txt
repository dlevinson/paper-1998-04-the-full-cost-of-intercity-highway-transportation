plans for the Federal Government thatPresident Clinton announced in December 1994 set thedirection for the future Department of Transportation.  Theseplans include reorganization and streamlining of the currentmodal structure into aviation, intermodal transportation andmaritime administrations; creation of an air trafficcorporation; and consolidation of categorical infrastructuregrants into a Unified Transportation InfrastructureInvestment Program.  Authorizing legislation will be proposedto establish the new organization and programs.  Budgetamendments, as required, will be submitted to support thesechanges.In the interim, pending these changes, the Budget in Briefpresents the Unified Transportation Infrastructure InvestmentProgram (UTIIP) and describes the balance of the Department'sprograms in the traditional modal structure.  The programsthat would be replaced by funding through the UTIIP areindicated.N.B.  Discussions are still in progress regarding programdesign and organization structure.=============================================================           DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION         HIGHLIGHTS OF THE FY 1996 BUDGET=============================================================The Fiscal Year 1996 budget request for the Department ofTransportation reflects the Administration's strongcommitment to making government work better and cost less.This budget's priorities are safety, investment andreinvention.An unprecedented re-assessment of the Department's purposeand the Federal role in transportation is under way and isdemonstrated throughout this budget:-  The budget requests $36.9 billion, down $2  billion netfrom the FY 1995 enacted level of $38.9  billion.  In total,infrastructure programs are funded at $24.9 billion, downfrom $27.5 billion in FY 1995, but still constitute 68percent of the Department's total budget.  Safety programsaccount for $2.1 billion, including safety grants to States,which increase 25 percent.-  The Department's ten individual modal administrations areproposed to be consolidated into a smaller organizationcomposed of aviation and intermodal transportationadministrations and the United States Coast Guard, therebyreducing bureaucratic redundancies and barriers to providingcross-modal efficiencies to the traveling public.-  Over 30 separate transportation infrastructure grantscurrently provided to State and local governments arereplaced by a Unified Allocation Grant that may be used forvarious transportation purposes.  The Grant will allow thoselevels of government that are closest to the users todetermine local transportation priorities and to fundinfrastructure of any modes that best meet theirrequirements.  It is anticipated, as a consequence of thisdevolution of authority, that there will be less reason tocome to Washington, DC for support of particular projects.-  State Infrastructure Banks are proposed to encourageinnovative and user-fee types of infrastructure financing tobetter leverage the Federal dollar and encourage privateinvestment.  A new Discretionary Grants activity will focuson projects of regional or national significance.-  The aspects of transportation that are a clear Federalinterest--providing mobility to enhance interstate commerce,safety, environmental quality, national security andtechnological progress--are key elements of the budget.-  The Department continues to reduce its workforce, inkeeping with the President's Executive orders and the FederalWorkforce Restructuring Act of 1994, and as many private-sector employers have.  This budget funds 6,000 fewercivilian employees than the FY 1993 DOT enacted budget.  Bythe end of 1996, DOT will be three-fourths of the way to the12-percent reduction goal to which the Department iscommitted by FY 1999.  Also, groundwork is being laid tocorporatize air traffic control operations in FY 1997, whichwill further reduce the DOT payroll by 40,000 employees.=============================================================                     DOT Reorganization=============================================================[Image]On February 2, 1995, Secretary Pena announced the proposedreorganization of the Department of Transportation from tenoperating administrations to three.The new organization plan is displayed here. This is $2.3billion below the comparable FY 1995 funding level.SAFETY-  Safety is central to all DOT activities.  The FY 1996budget request includes $2.1 billion for direct safetyprograms, such as inspections and safety grants.-  Safety inspector staffing is protected from cuts. In thearea of aviation safety, the budget requests an additional253 flight standards safety and certification inspectors, fora total work force of 3,315 by the end of FY 1996.  This is19 percent above FY 1994 levels.  These inspectors monitorthe airlines for compliance with aircraft safety standards.-  The request for Railroad Safety of $51.1 million is up 7percent from the FY 1995 enacted level and supports 409inspectors.  Motor carrier, hazardous materials, and pipelineinspectors are maintained at essentially FY 1995 levels.-  Safety grants to States increase 25 percent over FY 1995.This includes Highway Safety Grants ($196 million, up 29percent),  Motor Carrier Safety Grants ($85 million, up 15percent),  Pipeline Safety ($15 million, up 25 percent), andBoat Safety ($30 million, down 8 percent).ENVIRONMENT-  The fuel economy program is budgeted at $785 thousand,nearly double the funding level of FY 1995, to study emergingtechnologies that show promise of significantly improvingvehicle fuel economy in the next five to ten years.  Thebudget also requests $1.5 million for an environmental impactstatement to support a corporate average fuel economy (CAFE)rulemaking under consideration for model year 1998-2006 lighttrucks.-  Research funds support development of fuel cells andbattery operated transit buses, which would cut pollutionfrom the levels produced by conventional diesel buses.-  Congestion mitigation will be an area of emphasis underthe new $1-billion Discretionary Grants, established to allowthe Secretary to target funds to areas of particularimportance to carrying out the national interest  intransportation.-  DOT remains committed to cleaning up environmentalproblems for which it is directly responsible.  The budgetrequests $73 million for these efforts, primarily at FederalAviation Administration (FAA) and Coast Guard facilities.NATIONAL SECURITY-  A new maritime security program is proposed to supportnational security.  In exchange for payments over a ten-yearperiod, participating vessels will be available to theDepartment of Defense (DOD) to help meet sealiftrequirements.  The budget also proposes to increase fundingfor the maintenance and operation of the Ready Reserve Force(RRF)  from $150 million to $289 million.   An additional $70million is budgeted for RRF fleet acquisitions.  Although theRRF funds are budgeted in DOD, the Maritime Administration(MARAD) will continue to manage the program.  Continuedfunding for Title XI loan guarantees for shipbuilding willhelp finance modern ships and keep current our shipbuildingcapacity.TECHNOLOGY-  Technological progress is key to increasing efficiency ofthe transportation system and will mark the infrastructure ofthe 21st century.  Imagine the transportation infrastructureof 1900, vis-ˆ-vis paved roads, traffic control, airports andvehicles of today.  The infrastructure of the 21st centurywill likely be as much a departure from today as today isfrom 1900.  The Department's FY 1996 budget includes $956million in research, development and technology funding--a28-percent increase above FY 1995.  Of this total, $509million is funded in UTIIP.  Outside UTIIP, about $300million is devoted to aviation and maritime, and the balancesupports high-speed rail and various safety researchactivities.  Overall, the research, development, technologyand deployment priorities of DOT are consistent with those ofthe National Science and Technology Council.-  Funding for Intelligent Transportation Systems totals $356million, and includes implementation of "Trailblazer""--aprogram that will permit electronic clearance of trucks attoll stops and weigh scales.  The request also supportsdevelopment of real-time bus tracking through videoterminals, to improve bus fleet management and customerservice.-  High-speed rail technology development totals $68 million,up from $30 million in FY 1995.  Major elements includedevelopment of fuel cells for locomotives, a non-electrichigh-speed locomotive, signal and train control systems, andgrade crossing technologies.-  For the Partnership for a New Generation of Vehicles(PNGV), $5.25 million is requested.  Research will focus onimproving occupant safety and the crashworthiness of newvehicle designs and materials.-  Funding for Global Positioning Systems totals $113 millionat the FAA and Coast Guard, up 26 percent from FY 1995.-   For transit, $8 million is devoted to continueddevelopment of an Advanced Technology Transit Bus featuringlight-weight materials, advanced structural design, low fuelneeds and low emissions.-  Funding for multi-modal transportation research andtechnology coordination totals $7.6 million.  This includes$700 thousand to study the use of advanced materials intransportation and $1 million for technology deployment.-  The Department will be working closely with the Departmentof Energy on alternative power plants  for heavy-dutyvehicles, including rail and transit.=============================================================DOT should adopt a business-like approach to all programs,utilizing corporatization and privatization whereverpossible.=============================================================-  DOT has built into its FY 1996 budget 5-year outlaysavings of $6.4 billion associated with reinvention. Common services now performedby each mode can be shared or provided centrally.-  The President's budget request includes the elimination ofthe Interstate Commerce Commission. Therefore, DOTadds about 102 staff members and approximately $4.7 millionin funding to its FY 1996 budget.-  The budget request includes $331 million toward theacquisition of a consolidated headquarters building for theDepartment in FY 2000.  This will save taxpayer dollars incomparison to the current leasing of headquarters space fromvarious landlords.  The current Coast Guard headquartersbuilding will be vacated.-  The Department is committed to continue looking for waysto increase its efficiency and effectiveness.  New ways ofthinking, the introduction of time-saving technology, and anew openness to multi-modalism within the Department would beconsistent with the direction the transportation industry istaking toward, for instance, the development of intermodalfreight handling by ships, railroads and trucks.=============================================================The Department's assistance will be focused on capitalimprovements even in a reduced budget.=============================================================-  The budget for infrastructure, including FAA and CoastGuard facilities and Title XI shipbuilding loan guarantees,is $24.9 billion, or 68 percent of the FY 1996 budget. The account is structured in twoparts--State and locally initiated activities and Federallyinitiated activities--and reflects the Administration'sinitiative to shift programs to State and localdecisionmaking.  In future years it is expected that furtherconsolidation of separate activities and additionaldevolution to State and local governments would occur andthat the amount of the unified grant would increase.State and Local-  UTIIP includes a $10-billion Unified Allocation Grant thatwill be distributed by formula to States and localgovernments for them to spend according to theirtransportation priorities.-  UTIIP also includes $2 billion to capitalize StateInfrastructure Banks, which are expected to leverage anadditional $4 billion from bond markets.-  A separate funding category of $8 billion for InterstateHighways and the National Highway System reflects thenational interest in ensuring that the condition andperformance of these systems are maintained.  (Administrativeexpenses, research and statistics, previously funded in FHWA,are funded as a drawdown from this line.)-  Other funding includes $1.143 billion for priorcommitments for airport and transit projects, $500 millionfor transit operating assistance, and $10 million for RhodeIsland Rail  Development.Direct Federal-  UTIIP will contain $1 billion in Discretionary Grants,which the Secretary of Transportation will use to directfunds to projects of regional or national significance.-  A separate line for roads on Federal Lands is funded at$442 million.-  Rail funding of $1.035 billion will be devoted to Amtrak,the Northeast Corridor Improvement Program, and PennsylvaniaStation Redevelopment in New York City.-  Research and development includes funds of $113 millionfor Intelligent Transportation Systems and $106 million fortransit planning and research and university transportationcenter grants.  In the past, these programs were separatelybudgeted in FHWA and FTA.  Administrative expenses previouslybudgeted in FTA are also included here.=============================================================            FEDERAL HIGHWAY ADMINISTRATION=============================================================[Image]Under the Administration's reinvention proposal, theinfrastructure and related R&D programs of FHWA will bereplaced by funding through the Unified TransportationInfrastructure Investment Program (UTIIP). This is an increaseof $63.3 million (23 percent) over FY 1995, with theincreases occurring mainly in Section 402 grants.NHTSA's mission to improve traffic safety has a major impacton the Administration's efforts to reduce health care costs.Each year, health care-related costs of motor vehicle crashesamount to $14 billion, of which $3.7 billion is paid directlyby taxpayers.  To improve traffic safety and reduce healthcosts, the Secretary has announced two new goals to beachieved by 1997.  The first is to reduce alcohol-relatedtraffic fatalities to 43 percent of total traffic fatalities,down from 45 percent in 1992.  The second objective is toincrease safety belt use to 75 percent, up from 62 percent in1992.Highlights of the NHTSA budget include:-  For Operations and Research, $144 million, a 15-percentincrease over FY 1995.  Programs of significant interest inFY 1996 include $10.8 million to combat alcohol and drugimpaired driving, $6.4 million to encourage use of occupantprotection devices, $5.6 million to provide grants to 15communities to develop comprehensive local programs to reduceand prevent motor vehicle injuries, $1.6 million for"Patterns for Life" to promote child seats, and $2 millionfor the National Advanced Driving Simulator.-  $1.5 million for an environmental impact statement tosupport a significant corporate average fuel economy (CAFE)rulemaking being considered for model year 1998-2006 lighttrucks, $5 million for the Partnership for a New Generationof Vehicles, focusing on improving occupant safety andvehicle crashworthiness.-  For  Highway Traffic Safety Grants, an obligationlimitation of $196 million to States, the ISTEA-authorizedlevel for FY 1996, and an increase of $44.6 million from theFY 1995 enacted level.The request for Section 402 Formula Grants will fund localcommunity injury control programs through broad-basedpartnerships with the law enforcement, business, health andeducation communities.  Section 410 alcohol-impaired drivingcountermeasures grants would continue at $25 million, and theNational Driver Register would receive $2.4 million, a $1million decrease due to our plan to transfer two functionsto the American Association of Motor Vehicle Administrators.=============================================================              FEDERAL RAILROAD ADMINISTRATION=============================================================[Image]Under the Administration's reinvention proposal for DOT,infrastructure grant programs of the Federal RailroadAdministration (FRA) will be replaced by funding through theUnified Transportation Infrastructure Investment Program(UTIIP). A fundinglevel of $6.9 billion is proposed for the balance of FAA, ofwhich 70 percent is funded from the aviation account withinthe new unified trust fund.  Legislation will be proposed toestablish an air traffic corporation in FY 1997.Highlights of the FAA budget include:-  $4.7 billion for Operations, an increase of $121.5 million(2.7 percent) over FY 1995.  Inspector employment will beincreased over FY 1995 levels by an additional 253 flightstandards safety and certification inspectors--an 8-percentincrease that reflects the Department's commitment toaviation safety.The number of controllers and field maintenance technicianswill both decline about 1 percent, to 17,050 and 7,976respectively, due primarily to contracting out andstreamlining the operation of lower-level towers andrestructuring the airways facilities program.-  $1.908 billion for Facilities and Equipment (F&E), adecrease of $180 million (9 percent) from FY 1995.  Thebudget supports implementation of the Capital Investment Planby updating automation and older electronic equipment withnew solid-state equipment, and providing new facilities forthe modern equipment needed to handle the volume of airtraffic projected for the future.  The budget continuesfunding for major systems such as the enroute, terminal, andtower automation programs--formerly aggregated in theAdvanced Automation Program--and Voice Switching and ControlSystem, enroute and terminal weather radar programs, and GPS.-  $268 million for Research, Engineering, and Development(RE&D), an increase of $8.5 million (3 percent) over FY 1995.The research program is focused on improving the air trafficcontrol system by increasing its safety, security,productivity and capacity. The new unified programincludes $218 million to honor the FY 1996 payments forexisting airport letters of intent.   In addition, airportfunding is further augmented by the passenger facilitycharges (PFCs). The work of the Bureau consistsof compiling transportation statistics, implementing a long-term data collection program, issuing guidelines forinformation collection, coordinating information collection,and making statistics accessible.Under current law, financing of the Bureau's operations isauthorized as contract authority out of the Highway TrustFund.  The contract authority is included in the Federal-AidHighways program and is subject to the obligation limitationapplicable to that program.  Funds are transferred to theBureau from Federal-Aid Highways, where all obligations andoutlays are counted.The budget request provides for $20 million and 42 FTE. In FY1996, the Administration proposes to fund the BTS through theUnified Transportation Infrastructure Investment Program.Key activities of the BTS for FY 1996 include analysis anddevelopment of products from the commodity flow survey, thefirst half of data collection of the American travelpassenger flow survey, and compilation and analysis of stateand metropolitan area transportation data.  The two surveyswill be the only comprehensive sources of current informationon nationwide and border crossing movements of people andgoods.=============================================================                  OFFICE OF THE SECRETARY=============================================================[Image]The budget request for the Office of the Secretary (OST) is$578.4 million in FY 1996.
